We have surrounded ourselves with math and data lately, but the human brain evolved to handle small numbers only. Our short-term memory can hold [7 plus/minus 2](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/gilden/files/2016/04/MagicNumberSeven-Miller1956.pdf) digits, which is about the same, as the number of items we can grasp by just glancing at them. An ability called ‘**subitizing**’.

Confronted with larger numbers and more details, our brains start to rebel and save energy. Which corresponds with two types of ‘**numbing**’.

1. **Psychophysical numbing:** We become less sensitive to changes in larger numbers. While the jump from 10 to 20 feels significant, an equal jump from 340 to 350 does not.
2. **Psychic Numbing:** Our empathy diminishes as soon as we consider more than one victim. The fate of one individual feels weighty, but the suffering of thousands blurs into the abstract. Biased media uses this barbarously.

Our Stone Age brains favor concrete, whole objects, things we can count with our fingers and sometimes toes, too. If you're wondering why many number systems have the basis of 5 or 10, look no further than your own hands or feet.

Language hints in the same direction. While in many ancient cultures had names for small numbers, beyond 5 it often became just ‘lots’.

If you’re a person trying to communicate numerical data to your contemporaries, you have to consider that you're talking to their hunter-gatherer brains, which are averse to decimals, percentages, and numbers that do not lend themselves to a finger-and-toe count.

We often report too much detail forgetting most won't remember much of what we said. Experts occupied with specific problems are regularly beset with the ‘curse of knowledge’. They frequently think loud rather than communicating. And listeners lacking the context lose interest.

This is unfortunate since we usually want to convey something important fact with our numbers. Be it the virtual impossibility of dying in a plane crash ([0.000001\% chance](https://flyfright.com/plane-crash-statistics/)) or the outrageous costs of military equipment, like the $143 million for one high-end fighter jet. 

Without illustrating, contrasting or otherwise translating numbers to into the concrete, palpable domain of human thinking, these numbers will simply blur away.

Chip Heath and Karla Starr in their book _[Making numbers count: the art and science of communicating numbers](https://www.amazon.com/Making-Numbers-Count-Science-Communicating/dp/1982165448)_, present some fine methods to bring numbers to life,  

## Comparisons
One of the most effective ways of making numbers relatable is a comparison to a topic close to home. So is it a 1000 times more likely that you would die in a car crash on the way to the terminal than in an actual plane flight. 

Regarding the wildly expansive military equipment, instead of one F-22 Raptor fighter jet for \$143 Million you could buy 14,300 Feilong-300D Chinese suicide drones for $10,000 per piece. That is fourteen thousand and three hundredth drones versus one single fighter jet. 

Here are to more ways to get a sense of how much \$143 Million really are. You could vaccinate 3 million children with that money or build 4 apartment complexes could, providing homes for 400 families. If you think buying a jet is still worth it, know that $143 Million are just the ['fly-away' cost](https://militarymachine.com/f-22-cost), without R&D and not adjusted for inflation.

## Concretize
Another tip from Heath and Starr is to favor concrete number over abstract fractions. Saying 2 out of 5 is more concrete than $2/5$. Describing percentages like 0.4\% as baskets of concrete objects as in ‘2 in 500’ has the advantage of being imaginable. 

Sometimes it helps to cast data it into single chunks, like as one day, one game, or one person. ‘Every day, 50 people are murdered’ feels more real than ‘1 murder every 30 minutes’.???

If you cannot avoid fractions, ‘round with enthusiasm’ and have no more than two decimals.

To come to grips with large numbers, one can break them up into digestible morsels. For example convert the time into the unit of ‘lifetime spent’. Again translate lifetime spent into ‘money lost’, say for the time you spent stuck in traffic and the money you would have made spending that time working.
